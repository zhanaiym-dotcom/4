Research Question
Section: Question

Research Question

"What forms of public accountability occurred in Kazakhstan when public figures were publicly accused of gender-based violence between 2023 and 2026, and how can they be categorized as informal social sanctions, institutional/reputational sanctions, or formal legal consequences?"
  • Empirical Scope: Based on observable events, not normative judgments.
  • Defined Boundaries: Specific to Kazakhstan, public figures, and a defined time frame (2023–2026).
  • Analytical Goal: Answerable through the systematic categorization of documented cases.

Positioning
Section: Where This Sits

Epistemology & Positioning

Element Description
Epistemological Stance Descriptive
Justification This project does not seek to explain why accountability occurs (causal) or to test a hypothesis (positivist). Instead, it seeks to map and categorize the observable forms of accountability that emerge in the public sphere.
Researcher Positionality As an outside observer of these public events, I am not measuring the guilt or innocence of the accused, but rather documenting the social and institutional reactions to the accusations.
Analytical Goal To provide a clear typology of sanctions (informal, reputational, legal) that can be used for future comparative research in the region.

Contribution
Section: What It Adds

Contribution: What It Adds

  • Fills a Regional Gap: Most literature on gender-based violence accountability focuses on Western contexts. This project applies these concepts to Central Asia (Kazakhstan).
  • Provides a Typology: It moves beyond a binary of "legal vs. illegal" by explicitly recognizing informal social sanctions and reputational sanctions as legitimate forms of accountability.
  • Bridges Disciplines: Connects socio-legal studies with media and communication studies, showing how public accusations (often via social media) translate into tangible consequences.
  • Time-Specific: Captures a contemporary wave of digital activism and public discourse in Kazakhstan (2023–2026).

Literature
Section: What We Already Know

Literature Review: Three Camps

1. Informal Social Sanctions

Digital publics use shame, hashtags, and public call-outs to enforce social norms when formal institutions fail.

Arystanbek; Lewis & Christin; Hornle

2. Institutional/Reputational

Media framing and public pressure can lead to loss of sponsorships, job termination, or reputational damage for public figures.

Lewis & Christin; Meyer

3. Formal Legal Consequences

Legal frameworks in post-Soviet states often struggle to prosecute GBV, leading to low conviction rates and reliance on administrative penalties.

Kainazarova; Akisheva; Hornle

Gap
Section: What is Missing

Research Gap

What is missing?

While each of these sanction types is studied independently, there is a lack of integrated frameworks that categorize how they interact in non-Western, post-Soviet contexts.

  • Fragmentation: Studies tend to focus exclusively on either legal reform or digital activism, rarely both simultaneously.
  • Context Blindness: Theories developed in the US/EU do not always account for the specific political and cultural dynamics of Kazakhstan (e.g., the role of local Telegram channels vs. global platforms).
  • Lack of Typology: There is no clear, descriptive categorization of the forms of accountability that occur in this specific region and time frame.

Scope
Section: Limitations

Scope & Limitations: No Cause Claimed

This is a descriptive project, not a causal one. My goal is to map the landscape, not to explain the mechanisms behind it.

What I am doing

  • Identifying patterns of accountability.
  • Categorizing them into a typology.
  • Documenting the what and how.

What I am not doing

  • Claiming that social media causes legal change.
  • Testing a hypothesis about why institutions react.
  • Measuring the effectiveness of these sanctions.

Why this matters

  • Prevents overreach.
  • Keeps the focus on empirical observation.
  • Provides a foundation for future causal research.

Framework
Section: Typology

Framework / Typology

The framework is built on three mutually exclusive, yet often co-occurring, categories of accountability.

1. Informal Social

Definition: Actions taken by the public or digital communities.

  • Viral call-out posts.
  • Hashtag campaigns.
  • Public shaming on Telegram/Instagram.

2. Institutional/Reputational

Definition: Actions taken by organizations, employers, or media.

  • Loss of brand sponsorships.
  • Termination from public roles.
  • Negative media framing.

3. Formal Legal

Definition: Actions taken by the state or legal system.

  • Criminal charges filed.
  • Administrative fines.
  • Civil lawsuits.

Criteria
Section: Framework

Framework Criteria: Clarity, Comparability, Completeness

Why this typology works for a descriptive study:

Criteria Application to this Project
Clarity Each category has clear boundaries. A viral post is clearly distinct from a court ruling.
Comparability Allows for cross-case comparison. For example, comparing the consequences of two different accusations within the same time frame.
Completeness Captures the full spectrum of accountability, from the bottom-up (social) to the top-down (legal). It does not privilege one form over another.

Reflection
Section: Ungraded

Ungraded Reflection

What working with AI helped me understand

AI was excellent at helping me structure my thoughts and identify the boundaries of my categories. It acted as a sounding board to ensure my descriptive categories (social, institutional, legal) were distinct and comparable. It also helped me realize that my original RQ was too broad and needed to be scoped to a specific time and place.

What I still don't understand about my research design

I am still uncertain about how to handle the time-scope (2023–2026) in a descriptive study. Since the events are recent, will I have enough data by the time I complete the research? I am also unsure if I should treat attempted legal action (e.g., a police report that was dismissed) as a "formal legal consequence" or if it belongs in a different category. I need to refine my operational definitions further.
